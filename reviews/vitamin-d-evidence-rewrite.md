# Vitamin D, Sunscreen and Mood: What the Research Really Shows

*By Dr Gavin McAuley | EmpowerVida — proposed replacement for post ID 12; not yet published.*

Vitamin D is essential for healthy bones and calcium regulation. Researchers have also explored its effects on the immune system and brain. But a plausible biological mechanism is not the same as proof that extra vitamin D improves mood, prevents dementia or prolongs life.

## The landmark paper — and what it actually established

Michael Holick's influential 2007 *New England Journal of Medicine* review, *Vitamin D Deficiency*, drew attention to an important and sometimes overlooked clinical problem. It explained vitamin D metabolism, recognised causes of deficiency and established skeletal consequences. It also explored possible effects beyond bone health. It was a review, not a randomised trial showing that high blood levels of vitamin D improve depression or longevity.

In the skin, ultraviolet B (UVB) light initiates vitamin D synthesis. The liver converts vitamin D to 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the form usually measured in blood, and the kidneys and other tissues can convert it to the active hormone calcitriol. Vitamin D receptors occur in several tissues, including the nervous system. That makes brain-related hypotheses worth investigating, not proven clinical treatments.

## Is low vitamin D linked to depression?

Yes, observational research has found associations. Anglin and colleagues' 2013 systematic review and meta-analysis reported lower vitamin D status among people with depression. But association does not establish which came first. Depression itself can reduce outdoor activity, and health conditions, diet and other factors can influence both mood and vitamin D levels.

Experimental research has explored neurotransmitter and inflammatory pathways, but we should not claim that crossing a particular vitamin D threshold switches serotonin production off. Trials of vitamin D for depressive symptoms have yielded mixed findings. Vitamin D supplementation is not a substitute for proper assessment or evidence-based treatment of depression. Seasonal affective disorder is also linked to daylight and circadian rhythms; it is not simply vitamin D deficiency.

## What about sunscreen?

SPF50+ sunscreen substantially reduces UVB exposure under standard testing conditions, but that does not mean every regular sunscreen user will become deficient. Vitamin D status depends on skin pigmentation, location, season, clothing, diet, health and incidental exposure.

A particularly useful update is the Australian Sun-D randomised trial published in 2025. Among 639 participants, regular SPF50+ use was associated with lower average 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels and more deficiency than discretionary sunscreen use over about a year. This is meaningful evidence of a trade-off, but the study did **not** show that sunscreen causes depression. Its authors continued to recommend regular sunscreen use.

Australia has a substantial burden of UV-related skin cancer. Follow Cancer Council Australia's guidance, including sun protection when the UV Index reaches 3 or above. Do not deliberately seek sunburn or excessive unprotected UV exposure to raise vitamin D.

## Is there an ideal vitamin D blood level for everyone?

No universal target of 50–80 ng/mL (125–200 nmol/L) has been established for better mood, cognition or longevity. Different guidelines use different thresholds for deficiency or adequacy, depending on context. The Endocrine Society's 2024 guideline did not endorse routine blood testing or a specific preventive target for generally healthy adults. Testing may still be appropriate where deficiency is suspected or a person has relevant risk factors.

In Australia, results are usually expressed in nmol/L. To convert ng/mL to nmol/L, multiply by 2.5.

## Should everyone take D3, K2 and magnesium?

No. Supplements can be useful for people with deficiency or particular clinical indications, but doses should be individualised. D3 is commonly used, yet D2 is not universally ineffective. K2 is involved in calcium-related biology, but routine pairing with D3 has not been shown to prevent arterial calcification or improve outcomes in everyone. Magnesium participates in vitamin D metabolism, but that does not justify the claim that half the population needs magnesium supplements.

Routine vitamin D doses of 4,000–5,000 IU daily should not be recommended indiscriminately. Excess vitamin D can cause hypercalcaemia and other harms. Decisions should consider medical history, dietary intake, medications, risk factors and relevant testing.

## What should readers take away?

Holick's review was influential because it highlighted a genuine medical issue. Later studies have complicated the picture rather than establishing that more vitamin D is always better. Protect your skin from harmful UV exposure; consider vitamin D testing or supplementation when clinically indicated; and treat claims about mood, immunity and neuroprotection as questions that require stronger clinical evidence.
